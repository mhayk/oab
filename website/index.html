How it works

Four steps, no magic

  1. 01

    Give it your context

    Users, traffic, data, team size, budget, and what you cannot afford to lose. It asks at most five questions — only the ones whose answer changes the architecture.

  2. 02

    It works out the envelope

    Requests per second, storage growth, egress, connection demand, cost. Reproducible arithmetic from tested calculators — with the formula printed so you can check it by hand.

  3. 03

    It spends complexity deliberately

    Every component has an operational price, not just an invoice. A team of two can carry about four components before infrastructure starts eating the product. Over that line, something has to come out — or someone has to be hired, and it says which.

  4. 04

    Decisions come with an exit

    Every rejection carries the measurement that would reverse it. A “no” becomes a plan with a threshold instead of an opinion you have to trust.

The other failure

Not anti-complexity. Anti-unjustified complexity.

Kubernetes, Kafka and Redis are the right answer for plenty of systems. The question OAB asks is whether they are the right answer for this one — and it fails its own tests if it gets that wrong in either direction.

At 50,000 requests/second across three regions, OAB’s test suite fails if it does not recommend:

  • event streaming — 7,500 events/second with three independent consumer groups that must replay the same stream
  • a distributed cache — 67,500 reads/second cannot reach the database; at a 95% hit rate, 3,375 reach five replicas
  • a CDN — 1.04 PB/month of egress; 85% offload saves roughly $35,000/month, the single largest cost lever in the design
  • write partitioning — 7,500 writes/second exceeds one primary’s comfortable range

Scenario 03 in the evaluation suite asserts these components must be present. Two of the five scenarios guard against under-building; three against over-building.

Complexity is arithmetic, not advice

available = 4 + 1.5 × (engineers − 2) + 4 × dedicated_ops

Two developers get 4 points. A managed database costs 1; a self-managed orchestration platform costs 4. Over budget is rejected by default, and an override must name what is being dropped or who will operate the excess.

At roughly £240 per point per month in engineering attention, self-hosting a database to save £250/month costs about £720/month. The managed service is cheaper — said with arithmetic rather than preference.

What you run

Four commands

/oab:design

Describe what you are building. Get an architecture with the capacity numbers behind it, the components it refused, and the thresholds that would change each answer.

/oab:review

Point it at a repository. Get findings weighted by the scale it actually runs at — not a checklist borrowed from a system a thousand times larger.

/oab:capacity

Ask what a change costs before you make it. Requests per second, storage growth, egress and cost, with the formula and the substituted values printed.

/oab:adr

Record a decision with the options you weighed, the trade-offs you accepted, and the metric that would send you back to it.

A background skill also loads automatically, so ordinary architecture conversation gets the same proportionality — not only these four commands.
